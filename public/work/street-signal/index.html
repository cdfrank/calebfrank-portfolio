Capstone case study · November 18, 2026

Street Signal

Your city is the skatepark.

A product designer carried this game as a concept for a long time. When execution started, he directed AI the way a creative director runs a studio, and a skateboarding game built from real places came together fast. This is the case in motion: what was built, what was thrown out, and what is still unproven.

01 · The gap

Every skate game ships somebody else's city

Skaters already read architecture as terrain. The ledge behind the library and the three-stair at school are the real map. No game lets you skate the place you actually skate.

02 · Taste

The game got better every time the answer was no

AI agents produce fast and confidently. The founder's job was to say what was wrong. Three calls shaped the game.

Cut

Dice battles

The first design settled spots with a d20 game of S.K.A.T.E. It decided the outcome somewhere other than on the board. The rule that replaced it: the highest score holds the spot.

Rejected

The flat look

The first three style directions came back flat. All three were dropped as not game quality. The look that shipped is loud and built for a game: signal yellow, electric blue and black.

Rebuilt

The AI-built park

An AI workflow built the Ann Arbor Skatepark from its plan. Sizes and positions were mostly incorrect, and it rode badly. The response was two editors that snap a park to its evidence.

03 · Feel

A game ollie is twice as high, on purpose

Feel was measured before it was tuned. The game's ollie peaks at 0.64 m after 0.36 s. A real one peaks near 0.30 m after 0.25 s. A trick has to read on a five-inch screen, so it gets more air.

04 · Proof, including the bad news

Ledges are solved. Round rails are not.

Every mechanic has an automated test. On the Ann Arbor street course, a scripted rider approaches each feature 49 times. Each square is one attempt.

Grind lock-ins out of 49 approaches, and clean stair ollies out of 22. Tests prove the controls are consistent. They do not prove the game feels good to anyone but its maker.

05 · World

Three roads in, one format out

A spot can be scanned, traced from a plan, or modeled by hand. All three end in the same small set of parts, so one fix layer works on any map and the maps can move to a new engine.

The scan road works and is not optimized yet. Park dimensions in the modeled course are estimates from photographs.

Then a whole downtown

The biggest map is a city. Downtown Ann Arbor is skateable across a one-mile radius, built from USGS LiDAR and open map data. Pick a named drop-in point or tap anywhere on the map, and you are standing on that street.

06 · Map

The map is a product on its own

Signal Atlas puts a whole skate scene on one map. It answers what a skater asks before leaving the house: where can I skate, who is out, and which shop is open. This is the real thing, not a mockup.

  • Skate shops across Metro Detroit, each marked verified or call first, with phone, site and map links.
  • Parks, plazas and pump tracks, with a request to have a park built as a playable map.
  • Ten named drop-in points in Ann Arbor, from Liberty Plaza to the Diag.
  • Add a spot from the map, and switch the whole thing to night.

08 · Method

A studio with one employee

The founder is the creative director. Specialist AI roles propose, build and check the work, and they answer to one person with taste in both skating and design. The speed comes from the machines. The calls come from him.

One person at the centre. Everything else moves around the direction he sets.

  • Every claim is labelled by how much stands behind it.
  • Machine output is never treated as customer evidence.
  • Disagreements end as a decision by a person, with the options on the table.
  • Everything is tested before it is trusted.

10 · Now

It is playable today

Street Signal is open now. Anyone can log in and skate it in a browser, on a phone or a desktop. There is no launch date to wait for.

In the build

  • Two skateable downtowns: Ann Arbor and Detroit's Hart Plaza, each a one-mile radius.
  • Six places on the spot list: Test Park, A2 Skatepark, Cranbrook 11, Graffiti Box, Downtown A2 and Hart Plaza.
  • Touch, keyboard and controller play, with every trick and combo named on screen as you land it.
  • A running score and goals at each spot.
  • A world map behind the pause button, plus settings, mods and an in-game radio.

What the next stretch has to prove

  • A stranger picks it up on a phone, lands an ollie and locks a grind without instructions.
  • The game holds frame rate on one named mid-range phone, with the numbers published.
  • One spot goes from scan to playable on camera, with the cleanup time shown.

The story in one line

A product designer who helps run his city's skatepark nonprofit is directing AI like a studio to turn real streets and skate spots into a game, and he is publishing the failures alongside the build.

Street Signal is a skateboarding game built from real places. Players can skate downtown Ann Arbor and Detroit's Hart Plaza today, and scan their own spots to make them playable. It is being built in Ann Arbor, Michigan by one product designer who directs AI-assisted production himself. It is playable now at streetsignalgame.com.
